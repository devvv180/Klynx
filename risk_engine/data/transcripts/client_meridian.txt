Client: Rajiv Nair, CFO — Meridian Lifesciences Pvt. Ltd.
Date: March 12, 2026

Interviewer: Walk me through the business.

Rajiv: We make generic medicines — tablets, capsules, injectables — and two-thirds of 
revenue comes from exports. The US is 40% of total revenue. Europe is another 18%. 
Two manufacturing plants in Andhra Pradesh — Hyderabad and Vizag, both US regulator approved.

Interviewer: How is the business performing?

Rajiv: Margins are getting squeezed from multiple directions. Raw material costs are up 
because we import most of our key ingredients from China — suppliers we've worked with 
for years. The relationship is fine, but lead times have gotten unpredictable. Something 
has shifted in how they're prioritising their order books. We suspect domestic pressure 
on their end but can't confirm.

Freight has been the other big hit — logistics costs up over 20%. Our freight forwarder 
is giving us longer transit times and higher quotes. We used to plan 4 to 5 weeks from 
China to our warehouse. Now we quote 7 to 8 weeks internally just to be safe.

Interviewer: Do you know what's driving the freight disruption?

Rajiv: Not precisely. Our logistics team says certain shipping routes have become 
more expensive or are being avoided by carriers. We've just been absorbing the cost 
and adjusting safety stock upwards. That's created a working capital problem — 
now we hold more inventory than before. US buyers are also taking longer to pay. 
So we're squeezed on both sides of the balance sheet.

Interviewer: How is the regulatory situation?

Rajiv: It's our single biggest operational anxiety. We had an inspection at Vizag 
about 14 months ago — documentation and data management observations. We've addressed 
the findings, but until we get formal clearance, there's always a risk of action that 
could restrict US shipments. That's roughly 400 crore of revenue at risk if the worst 
happens. Our compliance team is on high alert.

Europe has introduced new rules around medicine traceability and environmental 
standards. Our contract clients there are passing those requirements down to us. 
In India, pricing controls on a few domestic categories hit our margins last revision.

Interviewer: Tell me about your technology infrastructure.

Rajiv: We're mid-transformation — vulnerable position. SAP on cloud for the ERP. 
But the manufacturing floor systems at Vizag are old — software the vendor no longer 
actively supports. We've had a couple of security incidents in the last two years. 
Nothing serious but a wake-up call.

The bigger worry is the US traceability requirement. Every product shipped to America 
must be tracked end-to-end. That requires our systems to connect with our distributors' 
systems. Every integration point is a potential vulnerability — and a problem there 
isn't just a technology issue, it's a regulatory one simultaneously.

We're also piloting an AI-based quality detection tool using edge computing hardware 
from an Asian vendor. Chosen on cost and performance. But there have been internal 
conversations about whether to revisit that given the broader technology environment.

Interviewer: People and talent?

Rajiv: R&D attrition has been painful — three senior scientists left this year, 
two to multinationals. The compensation gap is widening. The more concerning one was 
a regulatory affairs specialist who left just as we're entering an inspection cycle. 
At Vizag, we had a near-miss with a contractor workforce dispute six months ago — 
a reminder of how exposed we are if that export plant goes down even briefly.

Interviewer: Top risks in the next 12 months?

Rajiv: One — the regulatory situation with the US authority. Binary and immediate. 
Two — raw material supply from China. No easy fallback if a key supplier has a problem. 
Qualifying alternates takes 12 months. Three — a technology failure touching our 
compliance systems. A security incident that also triggers a regulatory response — 
that combination would be really bad.

Interviewer: What are you not paying enough attention to?

Rajiv: Our Vizag plant sits on the coast. We've had serious weather events in 
Andhra Pradesh — heavier rainfall, a flooding event near the port. Our business 
continuity plan hasn't been reviewed since 2023. It keeps getting pushed down the list.

The other one — and I say this as someone who doesn't track geopolitics closely — 
is that our business is quite exposed to how relationships between large countries 
evolve. We source from China, sell to America, our shipping goes through sensitive 
parts of the world. If any of those relationships deteriorate, we feel it through 
suppliers, freight costs, regulatory treatment. But we don't have a structured way 
to monitor that or build it into planning. We react rather than anticipate.

Interviewer: That's exactly what this assessment is designed to address. Thank you, Rajiv.
